Buying in London vs. buying in Canada: the 10 differences that actually matter

Rightmove looks like Realtor.ca. An "accepted offer" sounds like an accepted offer. It isn't. If you've bought a home in Canada before, here's exactly where that instinct will mislead you in England & Wales — and where it turns out to be a fair guide after all.

If you're buying property in England & Wales after buying in Canada, the biggest risk isn't a language barrier — it's a false-familiarity barrier. The two markets use enough of the same vocabulary — offers, agents, deposits, closing — that it's easy to assume the words mean the same thing. On several of the points below, they don't. Here's the system, stage by stage, next to what you already know from home.

Real estate rules vary by province in Canada, so where it's useful we've anchored the specifics to British Columbia — but the pattern holds across the country. This covers England & Wales, the system almost every relocating Canadian buyer will meet. Scotland and Northern Ireland run different legal systems entirely — Scotland's offers are binding within days via solicitors' "missives," for instance — so treat this as background only if you're looking there, and ask an advisor about the local rules.

1. Who's actually on your side

In England & Wales, the agent listing a property works for the seller — full stop. There's no MLS-style system where agents cooperate on a shared commission the way they do under CREA's MLS across Canada. Anyone can call themselves an "estate agent" here — no licence, exam, or regulator required, unlike Canada's provincially licensed agents (BCFSA in British Columbia, for example). Reputable firms instead join a voluntary scheme like The Property Ombudsman. If you want someone actually representing you, that's a buying agent, hired separately — and worth arranging before you view anything seriously, because without one, nobody in the deal is working for you.

2. Making an offer

In Canada, a written offer with subject clauses — financing, inspection, title — is close to a done deal once subjects are removed, days later. In England & Wales, an "accepted offer" is verbal or informal, usually just an email through the estate agent, with no standard contract yet and no deposit changing hands. It's a green light to start spending money on lawyers and surveys, not a firm deal. Nothing actually binds until exchange of contracts, commonly 6–12 weeks later — which is exactly why roughly one in four agreed sales in England & Wales falls through before completion. Send your written offer with proof of funds and a Decision in Principle (a soft, non-binding lending estimate), and confirm in writing what's included — built-ins stay by default, but curtains, appliances, and furniture don't unless you ask.

3. Chains, gazumping & gazundering

Because nothing binds until exchange, England & Wales carries two risks with no real Canadian parallel. "Gazumping" is a seller taking a better offer after accepting yours. "Gazundering" is the buyer's mirror version — cutting the offer right before exchange, when the seller has little recourse. Both are entirely legal. Most sales here also link several buyers and sellers into one dependent chain, where one weak link can collapse everyone's move — a real structural risk Canada's individual, financing-driven delays don't quite match. If you're chain-free (cash, first-time buyer), say so in your offer; it's a genuine selling point.

4. Mortgages as a non-resident

The mechanics look familiar. What changes is how much harder it gets once you're not a UK tax resident — which is most relocating buyers. Where Canada asks for a 35% minimum deposit from non-residents, England & Wales commonly asks for 25–40%, capped around 75–85% loan-to-value, and the lender pool shrinks to a short specialist list — HSBC UK, Skipton International, NatWest International, and Barclays among them, rather than the full high-street roster. A UK mortgage valuation, worth remembering, exists purely to protect the lender's loan — it was never meant to be a survey of the property for you. Budget 25–40% down as a non-resident, not the 5–10% headline figures aimed at UK residents, and use a whole-of-market broker with genuine access to non-resident lenders.

5. The survey nobody requires

Unlike a Canadian subject-to-inspection clause, nothing requires a UK buyer to commission a survey at all. Many first-time buyers skip it, rely on the lender's valuation alone, and find out about problems only after they already own the place. If you do commission one, it's an RICS-regulated chartered surveyor, with a standardised Level 1, 2, or 3 report and traffic-light condition ratings — Level 3 exists specifically for older, altered, or unusual properties, exactly the kind many relocators fall in love with. Get results back before exchange; after that, it's bought as seen. New builds still need an independent snagging inspection, separate from any RICS survey.

6. The legal process

This is the longest, quietest stretch of a UK purchase — typically 8–12 weeks of searches and enquiries between offer acceptance and exchange, handled by a solicitor or licensed conveyancer on each side, versus one lawyer (or notary, in provinces like BC) per side in Canada. With no cross-recognition between parties, an overseas buyer typically submits the same proof-of-funds documents three separate times — to the agent, the lender, and the solicitor, each running their own independent identity and source-of-funds checks. Instruct a solicitor the moment your offer is accepted, and arrange Power of Attorney early if you can't be in the UK to sign.

7. Exchange of contracts

Exchange is the moment that finally matches what "accepted" meant back home: solicitors formally swap signed contracts, a completion date is fixed, and — for the first time — withdrawing has real legal consequences. The deposit, usually 10% (sometimes 5%), transfers at this exact moment, and you're required to have buildings insurance in force from exchange, before you legally own the property, because risk passes to the buyer right then. Arrange insurance well ahead of exchange day, not on it — and remember that in a chain, one unready link delays everyone, even a fully ready buyer.

8. Completion & moving day

In Canada, closing day is a single event. In England & Wales, completion — moving day — follows exchange by 1–4 weeks, a distinct calendar event of its own. You legally own the property from the moment of completion, even though the official HM Land Registry record can lag by weeks or months catching up administratively. Keys release once your solicitor confirms funds arrived, not before, and your solicitor has 14 days after completion to file and pay Stamp Duty — a deadline that runs no matter how smoothly moving day goes.

9. Stamp duty & total costs

This is the one part of the system that already feels familiar: Stamp Duty Land Tax is a tiered, marginal-rate transfer tax, structurally the closest thing here to the land transfer tax you already pay at home (BC calls its version the Property Transfer Tax; other provinces use different names for the same idea). The standard bands run 0% to £125k, 2% to £250k, 5% to £925k, 10% to £1.5M, and 12% above; first-time buyers pay 0% to £300k and 5% on £300,001–£500,000. Non-residents face a 2% surcharge for spending under 183 days in the UK in the prior 12 months — worth noting against Canada's own foreign-buyer surcharges, which run as high as 20% in places like BC's Capital Regional District around Victoria, ten times higher than the UK's rate. Keeping a home in Canada while buying in the UK likely adds a further 5% surcharge on top. Beyond stamp duty, budget for conveyancing (roughly £1,500), a survey (roughly £300–£1,000+), and a broker fee.

10. Leasehold vs. condo ownership

Nearly every flat in England & Wales is sold leasehold, not freehold — the single hardest concept to translate for a Canadian condo owner, because the words sound similar but work very differently. A Canadian condo unit (a strata lot, if you're in BC) is owned outright, plus a share of common property, with no fixed term and one strata or condo fee. A leasehold flat here is a fixed-term right to occupy — often 99 to 999 years — with a freeholder owning the building and land, and both ground rent and a service charge, often paid to two different parties. A remaining lease term under roughly 80 years causes real mortgage problems, so always confirm freehold vs. leasehold immediately, and flag anything under ~90 years early, before it becomes a costly extension. Reform is moving through Parliament — a ground rent cap and a ban on new leasehold flats — but as of today, none of it is law yet.

Worth remembering: every stage above shares one theme — the UK system rewards buyers who have someone in their corner from the very first viewing, not just at the point of exchange. That's exactly the gap a dedicated buyer's advisor exists to close.
